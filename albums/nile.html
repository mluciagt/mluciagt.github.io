Denial is a river in Egypt

Aswan to Luxor, Egypt (April 2023)

The Nile stretches about 6,650 kilometres and is shared by eleven countries. Rising in the East African highlands, it winds north through Egypt before reaching the Mediterranean, feeding irrigation canals along the way and sustaining agriculture for millions of people. Egypt draws almost all of its freshwater, and part of its electricity, from the river, and a green ribbon of farmland runs through the desert on its banks.

That dependence is at the centre of a growing geopolitical tension. With Ethiopia building Africa's largest hydroelectric project, a step toward development and energy supply, Egypt and Sudan fear for their own water security. As a changing climate makes water ever less predictable, the question of who the river belongs to is becoming harder to avoid.

For those who live along it, the Nile is first of all a livelihood. Fishing, transportation of goods and tourism keep communities tied to its current. Captain Youssef spends his days sailing travellers between Aswan and Luxor, past the ruins of tombs and temples that line the banks. One of them, Kom Ombo, is associated with the crocodile god Sobek. Youssef told us that crocodiles are confined to the south of the Aswan Dam. Only after being reassured by his words did we jump into the water. On the sailboat, the wind sometimes disappeared altogether. Stuck in a windless stretch of the river, we watched ferry boats of all kinds pass by.
